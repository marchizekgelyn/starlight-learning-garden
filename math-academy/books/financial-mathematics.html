1. Interpreting percentages and annual interest rates

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

r = APR% / 100

A quoted APR of 6.5% becomes the decimal 0.065. Divide by 12 only when the calculation explicitly assumes a monthly nominal periodic rate. Never enter 0.065 into the Finance Lab APR box when you intend 6.5%; that field expects percentage points.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: 6.5%/12 = 0.5416667% per month for a nominal APR divided by twelve.

Important: A percentage can describe the percentage of a principal, the growth from one year to the next, or a rate of return. Always identify which denominator and time basis the percentage uses.

2. Principal, interest and ending balance

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Ending balance = Principal + Interest

Principal is the original invested or borrowed amount. Interest is the charge for borrowing or compensation for lending. The same cash flow can be an expense to one party and income to the other.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: a $10,000 loan with $1,500 accumulated interest has a total obligation of $11,500 before payments, fees and other adjustments.

Important: Separate the balance due from interest earned; using these labels interchangeably causes errors.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 03

3. Simple interest

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

I=P·r·t

Simple interest is computed on original principal rather than on previously earned interest. The duration t is measured in years when r is an annual rate. The ending amount, for no interim repayments, is P+I.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $10,000 at 5% for 3 years earns $1,500.00, reaching $11,500.00.

Important: Simple interest can model certain short-term loans; actual contract provisions determine timing and day-count.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 04

4. Simple interest over a number of days

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

I=P·r·(days/days_per_year)

For a fixed actual number of days, divide the annual period by the relevant convention—often 365 or 360. Financial agreements differ in whether they use actual/365, actual/360, 30/360 or another basis.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $5,000 at 8% simple interest over 90 days with 365-day basis gives $5,000×0.08×90/365 ≈ $98.63.

Important: Do not silently assume that every lender uses the same day-count convention.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 05

5. Compound interest

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

A=P(1+r/n)^(nt)

Compound interest accumulates interest on both principal and previously credited interest. The number of compounding events in a year is n. Keeping interest rates and frequencies consistent is essential.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $10,000 at 5% compounded monthly for 10 years becomes $16,470.09.

Important: If compounding frequency differs from contribution frequency, build an explicit timeline rather than using the wrong annuity formula.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 06

6. Daily, monthly, quarterly and annual compounding

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

n=365, 12, 4 or 1

With the same nominal APR and positive principal, a higher compounding frequency generally produces a slightly larger accumulated balance, if no fees or taxes apply. Monthly compounding uses n=12; quarterly uses n=4.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: compare compoundinterest(10000,5,1,1) with compoundinterest(10000,5,1,12).

Important: Banks may calculate daily interest but credit it monthly; the precise statement method controls actual accrual.

7. Continuous compound interest

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

A=Pe^(rt)

Continuous compounding is the limit of increasingly frequent periodic compounding, using the natural exponential constant e. It provides a useful mathematical model and is important in calculus and financial engineering.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $10,000 at 5% continuously for ten years becomes $16,487.21.

Important: This does not mean every commercial account compounds continuously.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 08

8. APR, APY and effective annual rate

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

APY=(1+APR/n)^n−1

Nominal annual percentage rate (APR) describes a rate convention. Effective annual yield (APY or EAR) incorporates compounding under fixed rates. For positive rates and more than one compounding event, effective yield exceeds nominal APR.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: 6% nominal APR compounded monthly corresponds to 6.168% effective annual yield.

Important: Consumer loan APR disclosures may incorporate specified finance charges, whereas this calculator’s nominal APR-to-EAR formula excludes fees.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 09

9. Converting annual rates into period rates

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Periodic nominal i=APR/(100·n)

When payments and compounding occur at the same frequency, converting an annual nominal rate into a periodic rate is a simple division. For an effective annual rate, the equivalent periodic rate is (1+EAR)^(1/n)−1, not simply EAR/n.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: an effective annual 6% return implies monthly effective rate (1.06)^(1/12)−1 ≈ 0.4868%.

Important: Do not divide the effective annual rate by twelve and call the result an exact monthly rate.

10. Present value and time value of money

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

PV=FV/(1+r)^t

A dollar today can be invested, so cash payments at different dates should be compared on a common date. Present value discounts a future amount by a chosen rate. Future value compounds a present amount.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $1,000 today invested at 7% annually for ten years grows to $1,000(1.07)^10.

Important: The discount rate is an assumption; higher rates reduce the present value of the same future payout.

11. Ordinary annuities and deposits

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

FV=C((1+i)^N−1)/i

An ordinary annuity pays or receives the same amount at the end of each period. The formula sums every contribution’s different compounding horizon. For an annuity due, when payments are made at the start of periods, multiply the contribution component by (1+i).

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: monthly deposits of $100 for 120 months at a 4% nominal APR use i=0.04/12 and N=120.

Important: An annuity calculation must state whether cash flows occur at the start or end of a period.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 12

12. Starting balance plus recurring deposits

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

FV=P(1+i)^N+C((1+i)^N−1)/i

Real savings planning often combines a lump sum with regular additions. Compound the opening amount over all periods and separately sum the contributions using the annuity factor.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $3,000 initially plus $250 monthly at 4.2% APR for ten years reaches $41,765.82.

Important: Projections assume a constant interest rate and do not account for tax, fees or market fluctuations.

13. Required deposit for a savings goal

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

C=[Goal−P(1+i)^N]/([(1+i)^N−1]/i)

Solve the future-value equation for the unknown monthly contribution C. If the opening balance already grows beyond the goal, the required additional contribution is zero.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: choose a goal of $25,000 after five years at 4.5% APR, starting with $1,000. Enter the values in Savings Goal and calculate.

Important: A shorter timeline increases the deposit needed when the other assumptions stay fixed.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 14

14. Fixed-rate loan installment

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

PMT=P·i/[1−(1+i)^(−N)]

For a standard fully amortizing loan, a fixed installment is designed so the principal falls to zero after N payments. The formula assumes fixed i each period and payments at period end. It excludes tax and insurance.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: a $240,000 loan at 6.5% nominal APR for 30 years has a monthly principal-and-interest payment of $1,516.96.

Important: Payment frequency must match the periodic rate; 30 years of monthly payments means N=360.

15. Reading an amortization table

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Interest_k = Balance_(k−1) × i

At each payment date, interest is calculated on the starting balance. The part of the payment exceeding interest reduces principal. The next row starts from the remaining principal.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: the first month’s interest on $240,000 at 6.5% nominal APR is $1,300.00. The first scheduled principal reduction is about $216.96.

Important: Earlier payments on a long amortizing loan often contain a larger interest portion.

16. Extra principal payments and payoff timing

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Paid = scheduled PMT + extra (capped at total due)

An additional amount paid directly against principal reduces the next period’s interest calculation. The scheduled contractual payment can remain the same while the loan ends earlier, unless the loan is formally recast.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: adding $150 monthly to the $240,000, 6.5%, 30-year example reduces the payoff period from 360 to 281 months in this model.

Important: Ask the lender whether extra payments are applied to principal and whether there are prepayment penalties.

New balance = old balance(1+APR/12)−payment

The debt-payoff tool assumes monthly nominal APR accrual, a constant payment, and no new transactions. It stops when the final payment clears the balance. Actual cards often use daily periodic rates and average daily balances.

18. Interest-only, negative amortization and balloon risks

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Principal change = payment−interest

Some credit contracts feature interest-only phases, low introductory payments or balloon amounts. A payment that fails to cover periodic interest produces negative amortization. These structures require their own cash-flow schedules.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $10,000 at 24% nominal APR incurs about $200 first-month interest; a fixed $100 payment does not repay principal.

Important: The fixed-amortization solver intentionally rejects a non-amortizing payment in its debt-payoff method.

19. Net present value of investments

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

NPV = Σ CF_t/(1+r)^t

NPV discounts all dated cash flows including the initial investment (often a negative time-zero amount) to a common present date. Positive NPV under the assumed rate suggests cash-flow value above that threshold.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: enter -10000,3500,3500,3500,3500 and an 8% discount rate in the NPV tool.

Important: NPV depends on both the cash-flow forecast and discount-rate choice; it is not an observed market value.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 20

20. Internal rate of return

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Find r where NPV(r)=0

IRR is a discount rate that zeroes the modeled net present value. Conventional cash flows with one sign change often have one IRR. Unconventional flows can have multiple real IRRs or none.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: pay $100 now, receive $110 in a year: IRR=10.000%.

Important: Compare IRR with NPV and project size; do not assume the largest IRR always produces the greatest value.

21. Compound annual growth rate

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

CAGR=(End/Start)^(1/years)−1

CAGR expresses beginning-to-end compounded growth as a smooth hypothetical annual rate. Intermediate contributions and distributions invalidate the simple two-point formula, so use cashflow-weighted methods where needed.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: $10,000 growing to $16,500 in five years has CAGR 10.534%.

Important: CAGR describes a smoothed rate, not the actual year-by-year investment path.

22. Inflation and real purchasing power

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Future price=Current price(1+inflation)^years

Inflation can reduce what a fixed dollar amount will purchase. The model can estimate a future nominal price or discount a fixed future nominal amount to today’s purchasing power.

Important: Inflation differs across categories, households and years; a constant rate is only a simplification.

23. Exact real rate versus nominal rate

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

1+real=(1+nominal)/(1+inflation)

Subtracting inflation from nominal return is a useful low-rate approximation. The exact relationship uses the ratio of gross growth factors.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: nominal return of 7% and inflation of 3% imply real rate 1.07/1.03−1 ≈ 3.8835%.

Important: When comparing purchasing power, always distinguish nominal and real values.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 24

24. Rules of 72 and 70

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

Doubling time≈72/annual-percent-rate

These mental shortcuts estimate the years required to double an amount under positive compounding. Their accuracy varies with interest rate. The exact annual compound doubling time is ln(2)/ln(1+r).

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: 6% annual growth gives approximate doubling time 72/6=12 years; the exact figure is about 11.90 years.

Important: Do not use this approximation to prepare contractual interest disclosures.

27. Interest through derivatives

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

d/dt Pe^(rt)=rPe^(rt)

Calculus describes the instantaneous rate of change of continuously compounded balances. The rate r times the current value is the instantaneous growth rate, so the balance satisfies dA/dt=rA.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: if A(t)=1000e^(0.05t), then A′(0)=50 currency units per year.

Important: Instantaneous change is not the same as the interest paid during an entire discrete month.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 28

28. Continuous streams and integrals

MATHEMATICAL MODEL

PV=∫₀ᵀ c(t)e^(−rt) dt

A continuous cashflow stream c(t) discounted continuously at r can be represented by a definite integral. This connects money to substitution, integration by parts, and the fundamental theorem of calculus.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Example: for constant c and positive r, PV=c(1−e^(−rT))/r.

Important: This is an analytic model; the deployed numerical integral calculator can explore the associated real-valued integrand.
